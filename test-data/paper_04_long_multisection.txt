Title: Cross-Lingual Transfer for Low-Resource Medical Triage Chatbots

Abstract
Medical triage chatbots that ask patients structured follow-up questions are
well studied in English but poorly supported in low-resource languages where
labeled triage dialogue data is scarce. We study whether a triage dialogue
policy trained primarily on English data can be transferred to five
low-resource languages (Swahili, Amharic, Quechua, Tagalog, and Khmer) using
a combination of multilingual sentence embeddings and a small amount of
in-language fine-tuning data (200 dialogues per language). We find that
transfer recovers 81% of the English-only triage accuracy on average across
the five languages, with the largest gap in Khmer, and that a simple
back-translation augmentation step closes roughly half of that remaining gap.

1. Introduction
Automated triage systems ask a patient a sequence of questions (chief
complaint, duration, severity, associated symptoms) and route them to an
appropriate level of care: self-care advice, scheduled appointment, urgent
care, or emergency services. Building such a system requires both a language
understanding component, to interpret free-text patient responses, and a
dialogue policy component, to decide the next question or the final routing
decision. Most published triage dialogue systems are trained and evaluated
in English, reflecting the availability of labeled clinical dialogue
datasets in English-speaking health systems. However, a large fraction of
global healthcare access gaps are concentrated in regions where the
dominant local languages are exactly the ones underrepresented in NLP
research. This creates a practical need for triage systems that work well in
low-resource languages without requiring the tens of thousands of labeled
dialogues typically used to train English systems from scratch.

Prior cross-lingual transfer work in NLP has shown that representations
learned on high-resource languages can transfer to low-resource ones for
tasks like classification and named entity recognition, but triage dialogue
policy is a sequential decision-making task, not a single-shot
classification task, and it is not obvious that the same transfer
techniques apply cleanly. Our contribution is an empirical study of exactly
this question in the medical triage setting, with a specific focus on how
much in-language data is actually needed to reach usable accuracy, since
that quantity directly determines whether a deployment in a new language is
feasible given realistic annotation budgets.

2. Related Work
Cross-lingual transfer learning has been studied extensively for text
classification and sequence labeling tasks using multilingual encoders such
as mBERT and XLM-R, generally showing that fine-tuning on a high-resource
source language and evaluating zero-shot on a target language recovers a
meaningful fraction of in-language performance, though with large variance
across language pairs and tasks. Separately, dialogue policy learning has
been studied mostly in monolingual English settings, using either supervised
imitation learning from expert-authored dialogues or reinforcement learning
against a user simulator. A small number of studies have looked at
multilingual task-oriented dialogue, primarily in booking and reservation
domains rather than medical triage, and typically assuming access to larger
in-language datasets (thousands of dialogues) than what is realistic for a
new low-resource-language medical deployment. To our knowledge, no prior
work has specifically measured how triage dialogue accuracy degrades, and
how much in-language data is needed to recover it, across a set of
genuinely low-resource languages spanning different language families and
scripts.

3. Data
We constructed a triage dialogue dataset in English containing 12,000
dialogues collected from a partner telehealth service, each ending in one of
four routing labels (self-care, scheduled appointment, urgent care,
emergency). For each of the five target languages (Swahili, Amharic,
Quechua, Tagalog, Khmer), we collected 200 new in-language dialogues using
local clinical partners following the same question schema as the English
data, plus a held-out in-language test set of 300 dialogues per language
that was never used for training or fine-tuning. We deliberately kept the
in-language training set small (200 dialogues) to reflect a realistic
annotation budget for a pilot deployment rather than a full dataset
collection effort.

4. Method
Our dialogue policy is a classifier over the next action (ask a specific
follow-up question, or emit one of the four routing labels) conditioned on
the dialogue history so far, encoded using a frozen multilingual sentence
encoder (LaBSE) followed by a small trainable transformer policy head. We
first train the policy head on the full 12,000-dialogue English dataset.
To transfer to a target language, we evaluate three conditions: (a)
zero-shot, applying the English-trained policy head directly to encoded
in-language dialogues with no further training; (b) fine-tuned, continuing
training of the policy head on the 200 in-language dialogues for that
target language; and (c) fine-tuned plus back-translation augmentation,
where we additionally generate 1,000 synthetic in-language dialogues by
back-translating English training dialogues into the target language and
include them alongside the 200 real in-language dialogues during
fine-tuning. Because the sentence encoder is frozen and shared across
languages, only the small policy head needs to be retrained per language,
keeping the in-language compute and data budget low.

5. Experimental Setup
For each of the five target languages and each of the three transfer
conditions, we report routing accuracy (does the final predicted routing
label match the clinically-labeled correct routing label) on the 300-dialogue
held-out in-language test set, and we report question-level accuracy
(does the predicted next question match the expert-authored reference
question at each turn) averaged across all turns in the test set. We also
report the same two metrics for the English-only policy evaluated on its
own held-out English test set, as the reference ceiling we are trying to
recover.

6. Results
The English-only reference policy reaches 91% routing accuracy and 84%
question-level accuracy on its own held-out English test set. In the
zero-shot condition, average routing accuracy across the five target
languages is 58% (range 41% Khmer to 69% Tagalog), which we consider too low
for deployment without further adaptation. Fine-tuning on the 200
in-language dialogues raises average routing accuracy to 74% (range 55%
Khmer to 83% Tagalog), recovering 81% of the English ceiling on average.
Adding back-translation augmentation on top of fine-tuning raises average
routing accuracy further to 79% (range 63% Khmer to 85% Tagalog), closing
roughly half of the remaining gap to the English ceiling. Khmer remained
the hardest target language under every condition, which we attribute
partly to the multilingual encoder's comparatively weaker Khmer
representations (confirmed via a separate retrieval probe) and partly to
greater typological distance from English question structure.

7. Error Analysis
Manual review of 50 misrouted dialogues per language showed two recurring
failure patterns. First, severity-related follow-up questions ("how bad is
the pain, on a scale of mild, moderate, severe") were frequently
mistranslated or misunderstood in back-translated training data, leading
the fine-tuned policy to under-weight severity signals in Khmer and Quechua
specifically. Second, in Tagalog and Swahili, code-switching with English
medical terms in real patient responses (e.g. a patient writing most of
their message in the local language but naming a symptom in English)
occasionally confused the frozen sentence encoder, which we did not
anticipate given that LaBSE is trained on predominantly monolingual
sentence pairs.

8. Limitations
This study covers five low-resource languages and a single clinical
partner's triage question schema and label taxonomy; results may not
transfer to different triage schemas, different clinical domains, or
languages typologically distant from the five studied here. Our
back-translation augmentation relies on an existing machine translation
system for each target language, and translation quality for Khmer and
Quechua is noticeably weaker than for Tagalog and Swahili, which likely
explains part of the remaining performance gap in those languages. We also
did not evaluate end-to-end patient-facing deployment, only offline accuracy
against expert-labeled reference dialogues, so real-world performance with
live patients, including effects of code-switching and dialect variation
not present in our collected data, remains untested. Finally, our 200
in-language dialogue budget was chosen for this study and was not itself
optimized; a formal data-efficiency curve (accuracy versus in-language
dialogue count) is left to future work.

9. Conclusion
Cross-lingual transfer with a frozen multilingual encoder and a small
trainable policy head, combined with a modest 200-dialogue in-language
fine-tuning set, recovers the large majority of English-level triage
accuracy across five low-resource languages, and back-translation
augmentation provides a further, cheaper-to-obtain improvement on top of
that. The remaining gap, concentrated in typologically distant and
lower-resource-translation languages such as Khmer, suggests that encoder
quality and translation quality, rather than policy architecture, are the
main current bottlenecks for further improvement.
